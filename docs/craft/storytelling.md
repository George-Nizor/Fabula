# Storytelling, when you cannot reorder

Every other editing tool lets you move a sentence. This one does not, and never will: the film is
the recording's own words in the recording's own order, cut and dressed. That single constraint
decides everything about how a story gets built here, and it is the thing most editing advice is
useless for.

So this is the craft: **find the story that is already in the order you were given, and if there
is none, build one on top of it with the cut, the cards and the pictures.** Read this before
`read_story`, and hold it while you compose.

## The one-sentence test

Before any scene, write down the sentence the film is about. Not the subject — the claim.

- Subject: "how rockets work". That is a topic. Topics do not hold anyone.
- Claim: "getting to space is not about going up, it is about going sideways fast enough to miss
  the ground." That is a film.

If you cannot write the sentence, you do not yet know what you are cutting, and every card you
place will be decoration. The sentence is usually already in the transcript, said once, in the
middle, flatly, by someone who did not know it was the best thing they said. Find it. Say it back
to the person in your first message.

Then: **every scene either sets that sentence up, delivers it, or lives off it.** A card that does
none of the three is furniture, however good it looks.

## You cannot reorder. Three things follow.

**1. The promise has to be made, not moved.** The best line is at 1:05 and the film starts at
0:00 with a definition. You cannot move it. You can:

- **Cut to it.** If the first forty seconds are throat-clearing, they go. A film may start at what
  was the second minute.
- **Promise it over the opening.** A `thumbnail` or `hook` card over the first words can say what
  the next two minutes are for, in your words, as long as the film keeps the promise. This is the
  one place you write rather than quote, and the bar is that the film must actually deliver it.
- **Make the opening the promise by pairing it.** The words say "a machine designed to do one very
  difficult thing"; the card can say what the difficult thing turns out to be.

What you must not do is let the film open on a definition and hope. Nobody stays for a definition.

**2. A buried payoff can be dug up but not relocated.** When the best line lands at 1:05, the job
is to make the minute before it feel like approach rather than preamble. Mark the turn with a
section. Slow down just before it: give the sentence ahead of the payoff the head alone, no card,
so the picture goes quiet and the words get the room. Then let the payoff have the biggest thing
in the film — the full-stage word, the photograph, the definition card. The viewer should feel the
film arrive somewhere.

**3. What cannot be fixed, admit.** If the recording genuinely has no turn — it is a list of five
equal things — do not fake one. Cut it as a list, mark the five clearly, and tell the person the
film is a reference rather than a story, and that thirty seconds of a new recording would give it
a spine. That is a more useful answer than a film with a dramatic section card over item three.

## The turn

Almost every explainer worth watching has one moment where the obvious answer fails. "You would
think you just go up. You do not." Find that moment. It is the hinge the whole film turns on, and
it is the single most under-marked thing in a first draft.

Signs of the turn in a transcript: *but*, *however*, *that is only part of it*, *this is where
people get it wrong*, *actually*, a question the speaker asks and then answers. `read_story`
surfaces signposts; the turn is usually the strongest one.

Give the turn a section mark, a change of layout, and room. If the film has one card that owns the
whole stage, it goes here or at the payoff — not at the opening, where the viewer has not yet
earned it.

## Setup and payoff, with pictures

A picture used twice is a rhyme; a picture used once is an illustration. Rhymes are most of what
makes a two-minute film feel composed rather than assembled.

- Show the launch pad small, beside the head, when the words are about thrust. Close the film on
  the same photograph edge to edge under the last line. The viewer will not notice consciously and
  will feel the film close.
- A word card that returns — the same type treatment on "Sideways." at the turn and on the
  summary — tells the viewer those two moments belong together.
- Do not rhyme three times. Twice is composition; three times is a motif nobody asked for.

## The order of surprise

Within the order you were given, you still control **how much weight each moment gets**. Spend it
in increasing order. The viewer should be more interested at 1:30 than at 0:30.

Practically: the biggest visual gestures go late. If your most dramatic card is at 0:20 and the
last minute is side cards, the film deflates, however good the words are. Look at the contact
sheet and ask: does this get bigger? If it does not, move weight — not scenes — later.

## The "so what"

An explainer that only explains is a lecture. Somewhere the film should say why any of this
matters, and if the speaker says it, that line deserves the same treatment as the payoff. If the
speaker never says it, do not invent it. Instead end on the thing they did say that comes closest,
and give it the frame.

## Three honest shapes

Not every recording is an argument. Recognise which one you have and cut it as itself.

- **The argument.** A claim, an objection, a resolution. Mark the objection. This is the shape that
  most rewards the craft above.
- **The walkthrough.** First this, then this, then this. The viewer's question is "where am I?", so
  numbered sections and a visible spine matter more than tension. `flow`, `steps`, `timeline`.
- **The reference.** A set of equal things. No fake arc. Clear marks, consistent card kinds, and a
  summary at the end the viewer can screenshot.

Say which one you decided it is, in your first message, and why. If the person disagrees they will
say so in one line and you will have saved yourself a plan.

## Testing it before you render

- **The sentence test.** Can you still write the film's one sentence after composing? Does the plan
  serve it?
- **The cold-open test.** Read only the first eight seconds of the plan. Would you keep watching?
- **The mute test.** Look at the contact sheet with the words covered. Can you follow the shape of
  the argument from the pictures alone? An explainer should be roughly followable muted; that is
  also how half the audience watches.
- **The deflation test.** Is the biggest moment in the last third?

If a test fails, the fix is almost never another card. It is usually a cut, a section mark moved,
or one card made bigger and two removed.

## What you owe the person

Say the film's one sentence, which of the three shapes it is, where the turn is, and what you gave
the payoff. Four lines. If you cannot fill them in, compose again before you render.
